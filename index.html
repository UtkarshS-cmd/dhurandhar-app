The Film

THE WORLD OF
DHURANDHAR

It begins with blood on the map. 1999. IC-814 hijacking. 2001. Attack on the Indian Parliament. India bleeds across a decade of terror — but from the shadows, a plan takes shape so audacious that even its architects dare not speak its name aloud.

Ajay Sanyal (R. Madhavan), Chief of India's Intelligence Bureau, is haunted by the nation's failures. In 2002, with approval from External Affairs Minister Devarat Kapoor (Akash Khurana), he launches Operation Dhurandhar — a decade-long covert infiltration of Karachi's criminal underworld and ISI nexus. His weapon: a raw, volatile 20-year-old Punjabi prisoner, jailed for a revenge crime, who has nothing to lose.

Assuming the identity of Hamza Ali Mazari from Balochistan, India's most dangerous ghost slips into the politically explosive neighbourhood of Lyari, Karachi — controlled by rival gangs, including the fearsome Rehman Balochi aka Rehman Dakait (Akshaye Khanna). Hamza gets a job at a juice shop owned by Aalam (Gaurav Gera), slowly earning trust, rising through ranks, and dismantling a terror network from within over a decade.

The story draws loose inspiration from the 2008 Mumbai attacks, the 1999 IC-814 hijacking, and Pakistan's real-life Operation Lyari — the government crackdown on Karachi's criminal syndicates. A mysterious traveller slips into the heart of Karachi's underbelly and rises with lethal precision, only to tear the notorious ISI-Underworld nexus apart from within.

Told in chapters across a 214-minute runtime — one of the longest Indian films in decades — Dhurandhar is the first instalment of a two-part saga. The sequel, Dhurandhar: The Revenge, releases 19 March 2026.

Principal photography spanned July 2024 to October 2025 — over 15 months across two continents and six Indian states and union territories. Director Aditya Dhar shot approximately seven hours of footage, crafting a saga so vast it became a two-part duology. Thailand's streets were dressed as Karachi; Indian villages became Pakistani settlements; industrial bridges became war zones.

🇹🇭 Bangkok, Thailand
The first and longest schedule — July 2024. Bangkok's dense urban landscape was meticulously transformed to recreate Karachi's Lyari district. Thai authorities were intrigued as Pakistani flags went up on set. The city's layered, gritty streets provided the most authentic visual substitute for Karachi available to the production.
🛕 Amritsar & Golden Temple, Punjab
November 2024. Shooting near India's most sacred Sikh shrine required extraordinary logistical sensitivity. Village settings near the Golden Temple were adapted with minimal dressing to portray Pakistani rural Punjab — causing a local stir when the tricolour came down and other flags were raised for a sequence.
🎬 Filmistan Studios, Mumbai
February–April 2025. The iconic Filmistan Studios became the production's nerve centre for intricate interior sequences, large-scale set constructions, and performance-driven scenes requiring precision cinematography unavailable in live-location shoots.
🌊 Madh Island & Mankoli Bridge, Mumbai
Madh Island's coastal isolation served outdoor sequences through April 2025. In May 2025, the Dombivli–Mankoli Bridge became the arena for several of the film's most explosive action set-pieces — physically demanding stunt work filmed with meticulous safety coordination over multiple days.
🏭 Golden Tobacco Factory, Vile Parle
July 2025. Ranveer Singh filmed a high-energy dance sequence at this iconic Mumbai industrial space — introducing a rare burst of colour and kinetic energy into Dhurandhar's otherwise smoke-and-shadow visual palette. The factory's brutalist industrial architecture creates a striking contrast to the film's espionage realism.
🏔️ Leh, Ladakh
From August 2025, high-altitude sequences around Patthar Sahib in Leh captured extremes of isolation and endurance. The crew battled altitude sickness and a mid-shoot food poisoning incident that briefly halted production entirely. Ranveer Singh completed his Ladakh portions by October 2025 — the final schedule of the entire shoot.
🏔️ Himachal Pradesh & Chandigarh
Emotionally charged sequences were filmed across Himachal Pradesh's valleys — the landscape doing narrative work through atmosphere alone. Chandigarh provided urban texture for scenes centred on the Punjab-origin characters whose lives straddle two nations and two identities throughout the decade-long story.

Dhurandhar represents the most ambitious Bollywood spy production in over a decade. Director Aditya Dhar, fresh from Uri: The Surgical Strike (2019) and Article 370 (2024), spent years developing the script — a densely researched espionage thriller rooted in real geopolitical events.

Principal photography ran from July 2024 to October 2025 — over 15 months across Thailand, Punjab, Maharashtra, Ladakh, Himachal Pradesh and Chandigarh. The production shot approximately seven hours of raw footage. The film was originally conceived as a single feature, but in October 2025, during post-production, Dhar realised the narrative's moral complexity and character depth could not be condensed without feeling rushed. The story was officially split into a two-part saga — both parts shot back-to-back.

The trailer was scheduled for 12 November 2025 but was postponed following the 2025 Delhi car explosion, releasing instead on 18 November at the Nita Mukesh Ambani Cultural Centre, Mumbai. The audio launch took place on 1 December 2025. At the 56th IFFI in Goa, Ranveer Singh received backlash for mimicking a sacred ritual, for which he later issued a formal public apology.

The film received CBFC's A certificate with a clean chit — officially declared a work of fiction not linked to any real officer's life. Soundtrack rights were acquired by T-Series for ₹45 crore, one of the highest music deals in Bollywood history. Dhurandhar is banned in Gulf countries, though it dominated overseas markets regardless — posting ₹42 crore internationally in its first 4 days alone.

The film crossed ₹1,000 crore worldwide on 26 December 2025, becoming the highest-grossing Indian film of 2025, the second highest-grossing Hindi film of all time, and the highest-grossing A-certified Indian film ever.
